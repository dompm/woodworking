Specs that are easy to get wrong are spelled out — arbor sizes, shank sizes,
hook angle. See [TOOLS.md](TOOLS.md) for what's already owned and why these
were chosen.

## Trip 1 — the document unit

Everything needed to build [projects/document-unit](projects/document-unit/).

**Already on hand:** wood screws (#8 × 1-1/4", for screwing the jig fences down
once the gap is dialled in — don't leave 24 cuts depending on a clamp that can
creep), and **6 clamps, which is enough**. Peak demand is 2 on the auxiliary
fence, 1 on the stop block, 2 on the jig and 1 holding the work — and bolting
the fence through the saw's own holes drops that to four in use. Shallow
throats are fine: the jig registers off the board's edge and clamps at its
overhanging ends, never in the middle of a panel.

### Lumber

- [ ] **1x12 pine × 4**, 8 ft — 18 linear feet needed, so three is exactly
      enough and the fourth is insurance against a knot in the wrong place.
      **1x12, not 1x10:** a letter duo-tang is 9 to 9-1/2" wide, and a 1x10's
      9-1/4" actual is too tight for the wide ones. Ripped to a common **11"**.
      **Buy flat boards — this is now your main defence**, since there's no
      week of acclimation. Straightedge across the width, sight down the
      length, reject anything cupped or crooked. Sticker them at home
      overnight regardless.

### Sheet goods

- [ ] **3/4" MDF handy panel, 2 × 4 ft** — does the jig rails, the crossbar,
      the mitre saw auxiliary fence and the stop blocks. One panel covers all
      of it.
- [ ] ~~Second panel for a sacrificial backer~~ — **not needed.** The mitre saw
      does the cutting to length, so the only router cuts that go through are
      the ten lap slots: a 3/4" × 5-1/2" window each. A 6" × 12" scrap under
      that patch is enough, and you'll have ~14 linear feet of 1x12 offcut.
      Pine also avoids adding to the MDF dust you'll already be making on the
      jig.

### Blades

- [ ] ~~10" mitre blade, 60T ATB~~ — **defer.** The saw's blade guard doesn't
      retract, so it's unusable until that's fixed, and if the parts get routed
      to final length the mitre saw only makes rough cuts — where the existing
      rusty blade is fine. Buy it later if the guard fix works and you want the
      speed. When you do: must say **"sliding"** or
      **"compound mitre saw"** on the packaging. A blade that doesn't specify
      is probably positive-hook, which self-feeds; on a slider that means the
      head lunges toward you mid-cut. This is a safety spec, not a finish one.
- [ ] **7-1/4" circular blade, 40T, 5/8" arbor** — 40T rather than a coarse
      24T ripper because that ripped edge becomes the visible front edge of
      the case.

### Router

- [ ] **1/2" straight bit, two-flute carbide, 1/2" shank** — this one bit cuts
      all 24 joints in the document unit.
- [ ] **1/2" flush trim bit** (Diablo 1/2 × 1-1/2 is fine) — **only if the
      mitre saw stays broken.** Trims parts to final length against a template
      board: clamp a correctly-cut board under the oversize one, bearing rides
      it, cutter trims flush. Every part comes out matching the same physical
      template rather than a stop block that can drift.
      **Check where the bearing sits.** At the tip = flush trim, template
      underneath. Above the cutter = pattern bit, template on top. The name is
      used loosely and the two need opposite setups. With the template
      underneath the shank never enters the cut, so 1/2" shank is fine — and
      stiffer.

### Glue, finish and abrasives

- [ ] **Shellac — Zinsser SealCoat, QUART (946 ml), not the 3.7 L gallon.**
      The piece is ~38 sq ft of surface; three coats is ~115 sq ft of coverage
      and a quart covers well past that. The gallon is ~$124 and would go off
      in the can before you used it — old shellac never fully hardens and
      stays tacky. **Check the date code before buying.**
      If SealCoat isn't in stock: Bulls Eye Clear in a quart (3 lb cut, so
      wipe thin or thin it ~25%), or water-based poly. **Not** anything
      oil-based — see below.
      SealCoat is a dewaxed
      2 lb cut that wipes on straight from the can; Bulls Eye is a 3 lb cut
      that streaks unless thinned, which is the only reason you'd need a
      solvent at all. Dewaxed also means anything can go over it later.
      Needed *during* the build, not after: with ten enclosed cells it is far
      easier to finish the parts flat and mask the joints than to reach into
      12 × 3-1/2 openings afterwards.
      **Do not use an oil finish on this piece.** Boiled linseed and Danish oil
      off-gas for weeks, and this one lives in a closed closet full of paper —
      the smell transfers. Shellac's alcohol flashes off in minutes and it is
      inert after that. It also dries in 20 minutes, so three coats is an
      evening, and it wipes off with alcohol if you hate it.
- [ ] ~~Denatured alcohol~~ — **not needed with SealCoat.** And note it is not
      rubbing alcohol: shellac wants ethanol or methanol, and the water in 70%
      isopropyl makes it blush. In Canada it's sold as **methyl hydrate**.
- [ ] ~~Titebond III~~ — **already have it: Gorilla WOOD Glue.** Its ~10-15 min
      open time matches or beats Titebond III, and beats the Titebond II that
      Home Depot stocks when III is out. Check the bottle pours smoothly —
      stringy or separated PVA is dead, as is anything that's been frozen.
      **Not** original Gorilla Glue (brown polyurethane): moisture-cured,
      foams, and the foam has no strength.
- [ ] **Sander discs — 150 and 180 only.** The boards arrive planed, so the
      only defects are mill marks and fuzz, which is 150 work; 120 is for
      removing material and you have none to remove. **Check first:** rake
      light across a face — visible ripples mean deep mill marks and 120 is
      worth having, a flat dull surface means start at 150. Never skip more
      than one step (120 → 220 leaves scratches that only appear under finish).
      **Take the sander (or a used disc) to the store**: diameter (5" or 6"), hole count (5 / 8 / multi-hole) and
      backing (hook-and-loop vs peel-and-stick) all have to match. A wrong hole
      pattern still sticks but kills dust extraction, so the disc clogs and
      burns.
- [ ] **Sandpaper sheets, 150 and 180** — for what the sander can't reach.
      180 alone does the arrises on already-sanded faces, but **the ripped
      edges need 150 first**: a 40T circular blade leaves marks that 180 only
      polishes around, and those edges are the visible front and back of every
      part. Use a sanding block there or you'll round them over.
      **Do not sand the housings or lap slots** — they're glue surfaces, and
      router-cut is already flatter than sanded. Sanding rounds the shoulders
      and opens the fit.
- [ ] **320 or 400 sheets**, or a fine abrasive pad — a very light scuff
      between shellac coats.

Stop at 180 on pine. Finer burnishes the surface, it stops absorbing evenly and
the finish goes patchy.

### Hardware and tools

- [ ] **Combination square** — preferred over a try square: same job, plus
      depth setting and marking. Gets used constantly.
- [ ] **Painter's tape** — glue-up cleanup, marking, holding things

### Safety

- [ ] **N95 dust mask** — routing MDF throws genuinely nasty fine dust
- [ ] **Ear muffs** — the router runs ~100 dB, where safe exposure is about 15
      minutes a day. Muffs rather than plugs: worse acoustically, but you'll be
      pulling them on and off between every cut, and anything fiddly gets
      skipped.
      **Noise-cancelling earbuds do not count.** ANC works below ~1 kHz; a
      router's damage is high-frequency, so all you'd get is the passive seal —
      uncertified, and you'd end up turning music up over the tool, which adds
      energy rather than removing it. If you want audio, **3M WorkTunes** (~$60,
      rated, volume-limited) or ISOtunes buds.

Safety glasses: already owned.

### ⚠️ Decide at the store

**What width to rip the boards to.** They're 9-1/4" actual and every board has
to end up the same width or the egg-crate laps won't sit flush. If they're
clean, 9-1/8"; if any edge is rough or a board is cupped, drop to 9". The lap
slot depth is exactly half whatever you land on — **write the number down**, it
changes the model and the cut list.

### ⚠️ Before spending anything

Plug the mitre saw in. Check the slide runs freely and there's no side-to-side
play in the head. Fifteen years in a shed is hard on the parts that aren't the
blade, and if it's seized the cut-to-length plan changes and so does this list.

## Later — not needed for the document unit

### Footrest ([projects/footrest](projects/footrest/))

- [ ] **Walnut or poplar** — one board at least 11" wide × 17" long, plus
      sides. About 2-1/2 board feet. **Ask what width they actually stock
      before committing to the design** — under 11" and the depth has to come
      down.
- [ ] **1/4" roundover bit** — the generous soft edge is most of what makes it
      read as furniture
- [ ] **Navy felt**, 13" × 4", plus spray adhesive
- [ ] **Non-slip pads** — rubber or cork, not felt. A brace that slides across
      the floor solves nothing.
- [ ] Gel stain or dye if going the poplar route — **not** a pigment stain,
      which blotches badly on poplar

### Salt boxes ([projects/salt-boxes](projects/salt-boxes/))

- [ ] **Hardwood, about a foot of 4/4** — everything is 3/4" so it's one board.
      Maple takes a chamfer crisply; cherry darkens with handling.
- [ ] **1/2" pattern bit (top bearing)** — the one job where it's the right
      tool, because an interior opening can't be guided by fences
- [ ] **1/4" hardboard** for the template frame
- [ ] **Double-sided tape** — how small template work gets held
- [ ] **Brass or stainless screws, #6** — never plain steel; salt rusts it and
      it bleeds a black stain into the wood
- [ ] **6mm × 3mm disc magnets** and small steel discs
- [ ] **Epoxy** — to cap the magnets, since salt eats the nickel plating
- [ ] **Mineral oil and beeswax** — food contact, and renewable

### Whenever

- [ ] **Four 36" clutch-style bar clamps**, steel bar, known brand (Bessey,
      Jorgensen, Pony). Not needed until there's a panel to glue — the
      document unit gets clamped with the ratchet straps. Check used listings
      first; clamps have no wear parts.
