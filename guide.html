Shop build guide

Mid-century wine rack

Twelve bottles in walnut or cherry, on splayed tapered legs. Written for a table saw, router, mitre saw and hand tools — and for someone whose last project was a picture frame.

  • Finished 10-1/4 × 9 × 39-1/2 in
  • Openings 4 in square
  • Stock 3/4 in, 9 in wide
  • Material ~18 linear ft

What it is

The five shelves are housed into the stiles. The interior grid is a half-lapped egg crate — one continuous divider notched into the shelves where they cross. At the top and bottom the rails land in a rabbet, so the outside of the case shows nothing but two continuous vertical lines.

01

Lock the two things you don't control

Both of these decide dimensions downstream, so settle them before any wood is cut.

Buy the legs first. You want 10″ tapered legs with brass ferrules and 8° angled mounting plates. Check the plates are angled — plenty of sellers ship flat 0° ones by default. A 10″ leg gives 39-1/2″ overall.

Ask what board width your yard actually stocks, surfaced. Whatever you get becomes the case depth. Don't design around 9″ and then find out.

The one thing to get right

Every part uses the full width of the board, and those edges become the visible front and back edges of the case. So all your stock has to finish at exactly the same width.

02

Bring every board to one width

With no jointer: screw or tape each board to a straight strip of plywood, run the plywood against the table saw fence, and the blade gives you a dead-straight edge. Then flip that edge to the fence and rip every board to final width at one fence setting. Don't move the fence between boards.

Check three of them with calipers. They must match. A board 1/64″ narrow means a shelf sits proud at the front of the case permanently.

03

Cut the parts to length

Mitre saw with a stop block, or a crosscut sled. Never measure each piece individually.

The seven B parts set the case width. If they differ from each other the case racks and no amount of clamping fixes it. One stop setting, seven cuts.

Label every part in pencil, and mark which edge is the FRONT. That matters in step 8 and it is not recoverable later.

Cut two extra offcuts for test joints. Don't skip them.

04

Measure your actual stock thickness

Put calipers on your “3/4 inch” boards. They will read something like 0.72″ or 0.74″. Write that number on the wall. Every housing you cut has to match it, not the nominal 3/4″.

Most common way first projects go wrong

Cutting housings to nominal 3/4″ when the wood is 0.72″. Every joint in the piece ends up sloppy and there is no fixing it afterwards.

05

Build the T-square jig

A strip of 1/2″ plywood about 30″ long, with a shorter piece fixed across one end at 90°. The long arm is the fence the router rides; the short arm hooks over the edge of the board, which is what makes the cut square without measuring an angle.

The housing has to match your stock, not a number. Two ways to guarantee that; the first is much easier to set up.

Option A — pattern bit and template

A pattern bit (top-bearing straight bit) has a ball bearing above the cutter, made to exactly the cutter's diameter. So the cut reproduces the template one-to-one, with no offset at all — which means the gap in your jig simply is the housing.

Set it with the board itself

Clamp the two rails against a piece of your actual shelf stock, tighten, pull the stock out. The jig now cuts a housing that fits that stock. No arithmetic, no router base diameter, nothing measured.

The one dimension to get right: template thickness ≈ cutter length − cut depth, so the bearing lands within the template's thickness. With a typical 1″ cutter and a 1/4″ deep housing that's 3/4″ ply or MDF, not 1/2″. Too thin and the bearing rides above the template and guides nothing.

Run the bearing against one rail, then back along the other. Add a crossbar at 90° so the jig registers square to the edge.

Option B — two fences, no special bit

If you'd rather not buy a pattern bit, capture the router base between two fences instead. Same idea, but now the base diameter enters the arithmetic:

gap between fences = width + baseØ − bitØ
router free travel  = width − bitØ

With a 6″ base and a 1/2″ bit on 3/4″ stock: a 6-1/4″ gap and 1/4″ of travel. Make one fence adjustable, then dial it in with test cuts in scrap.

Mark the base and keep it facing one way

This version references opposite sides of the router base, so if the base isn't perfectly concentric with the bit, that error goes straight into the width. Keeping the same part of the base against each fence makes the error constant, and your test cuts then absorb it. The pattern bit has no such problem — its bearing is concentric by construction.

You are not trying to hit 0.750 inches

You are matching your own stock. If your boards measure 0.727″, a “perfect” 0.750″ housing is a bad housing. The target is a feel: slides in with firm hand pressure, no mallet.

06

Cut test joints in real offcuts

Half an hour here saves the project. Pine will lie to you about fit — it crushes. Use walnut or cherry offcuts.

  • A housing plus a shelf end. Creep the second pass until an offcut slides in with hand pressure and no mallet.
  • A rabbet at a board end. Same setup, run off the end.
  • One lap crossing. The only joint here you haven't done before.

Write the final jig offset on the jig in pencil, then stop.

07

Rout the seven housings in each stile

08

Cut the ten lap slots

All 3/4″ wide × 4-1/2″ deep, cut in from an edge, all the way through the thickness. Five in the shelves, five in the divider.

Three or four depth passes — don't try to hog 3/4″ of walnut in one go. Cut the slot a hair fat so the parts go together without forcing.

Why not the table saw

4-1/2″ is past a 10″ blade's reach, and stopping a crosscut mid-board leaves the blade's arc cutting far past your line on the underside.

09

Housings for the divider ends

Only the top and bottom rails get these — the five shelves don't, because the lap does that job there.

3/4″ wide × 3/16″ deep, through, centred 4-1/4″ to 5″ from either end. Bottom rail on its top face only; top rail on its bottom face only.

10

Dry fit — twice

First just the egg crate. Stand the five shelves up in order, line the divider up behind them with its notches on the shelves, and push it forward 4-1/2″ until everything is flush. It goes together by hand and stands square on its own.

The divider cannot drop in from above

It is full depth everywhere except at its five notches, and a full-depth section can't pass a shelf — the shelf's slot only removes the back half. Assemble the grid with a horizontal slide, never a vertical drop.

Measure the openings while it's dry; they should all be 4″.

Then the whole carcass, dry, no glue. Measure the diagonals of the front face — they must match. Fix problems now.

11

Glue up, in two stages

Do it in two stages. Landing fourteen housings and ten lap joints in one go is the most stressful glue-up in the project, and it is avoidable. Use Titebond Extend or liquid hide glue — you want the open time.

Stage one — the egg crate on its own. Glue the ten lap faces lightly, slide the divider forward into the shelves as you did dry, square it up, clamp lightly and let it cure. You now have one rigid grid instead of six loose parts.

Stage two — the carcass.

  • Thin glue in the housing, not on the shelf end — less squeeze-out to clean.
  • One clamp per pair of shelves, with cauls. Snug, not crushing.
  • Check the diagonals and rack it square before the glue grabs.
  • Wipe squeeze-out inside the cells now. Dried glue blocks finish and shows as pale patches.

Leave it overnight.

12

Base, then finish

  • Glue a 3/4 × 1-1/2 cleat along the front and back edges of the bottom cell floor, inside. It doubles the screw purchase and is invisible from outside.
  • Screw the plates to the underside, set in 3/8″ from the edges, each rotated 45° in plan so its angle leans out toward the nearest corner.
  • Screw in the legs. If it rocks, trim the long foot flat and parallel to the floor — not square to the leg.
  • Sand 120 → 180 → 220, breaking every sharp edge. Hardwax oil or wiping varnish, two or three thin coats.

Cherry keeps darkening for about a year. That's normal, and it's the good part.

!

The five things most likely to go wrong

None of these are skill problems. They are all preventable with setup.

  1. Boards not all the same width. Shelves sit proud or shy at the front. Fixed in step 2, nowhere else.
  2. Housings cut to nominal 3/4″ instead of your real thickness. Sloppy joints throughout. Step 4.
  3. Stiles routed separately. Shelves land at slightly different heights left to right and the whole grid looks off. Step 7 — clamp them together.
  4. Lap slots cut from the wrong edge. The grid won't assemble. Step 8.
  5. Rushing the glue-up. Long-open-time glue, and dry fit twice.

Not a weekend. Milling and cutting to size is an evening; jigs and test joints another; the joinery is the bulk of it; then glue-up plus overnight, and finishing spread over a few days. Take the joinery slowly — everything else is forgiving and that part isn't.
